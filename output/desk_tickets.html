1. How I evaluate the agents on each ticket

Ticket 101: Tauhid Zaman's Bulldog tee. Grade: good, with one judgement call I'd question and one rule slip.

The diagnosis was exactly right. Inventory used check_bulk_order to find 0 white tees in size S and a shortfall of 1. It then used get_invoice_status(501) and list_vendors to see that Bulldog Print Co, the only apparel vendor, couldn't ship because invoice 501 ($840, due 2026-08-28) was 3 days overdue. Inventory handed the money question to Accounting instead of answering it itself, which is the division of labour I designed.

Accounting went further than I expected. It queued approval #1, the $840 invoice payment (prepare_payment), and approval #2, an $8 purchase order for one tee (prepare_purchase_order). The second one was flagged as blocked until #1 was paid, and the code enforced that order when I approved them. The Boss checked list_approvals and set the ticket to waiting_approval. Customer Service saved draft 1, which explains the shortage and a conditional 2026-09-05 arrival. Nothing was sent. I approved both payments, so the ticket cost the shop $848.

I'm of two minds about the $8 order. From the agents' side it was the logical next step: paying the invoice unblocks the vendor, but on its face it doesn't put a tee on the shelf. As a business owner, though, I notice that invoice 501 is described as "Rush reprint CC-TEE-WHITE S". An $840 bill for a size S reprint suggests that order may already cover this tee, so the $8 order could be a duplicate. The agents had no tool that shows what an invoice contains beyond its description, so they couldn't check. It was cheap, but it's the kind of small, plausible spend that adds up when nobody verifies it. Separately, draft 1 tells the customer about "pending human approvals for overdue invoice 501". The Customer Service prompt says to keep that internal. That's why drafts stay drafts until a human reads them.

The failed first attempt (run 536067aaa28e, before the reset I counted) taught me the most. Every agent identified the right action: Accounting wrote that "invoice payment should be prioritized", and the Boss's final call said to "prioritize invoice 501". But no one called prepare_payment, so the approval queue stayed empty and the ticket ended in_progress with nothing for me to approve. A correct recommendation is not the same as a completed tool action. In a multi-agent system the gap is easy to miss, because a confident summary reads like work that has been done. The real record of work is the database: here, an approvals row. I fixed it two ways. Accounting's prompt now says to queue needed payments "even if you were only asked to advise". The Boss must check list_approvals before closing a ticket and send any missing payment back. The second attempt queued both approvals.

Ticket 102: Chapel Street rent. Grade: accurate and financially aware, but more process than it needed.

Facilities called get_rent_due(1) and got everything right: $2,400 to Elm City Properties, due 2026-09-02, 2 days after the shop date, so due soon and not overdue. Accounting then did the part I care about most. It didn't just check that $3,400 covers $2,400. It counted the $848 already promised to ticket 101 (approvals #1 and #2) and reported that paying everything pending would leave $152. It queued approval #3 with prepare_payment(kind=rent). The Boss set the ticket to waiting_approval. I approved #3, cash dropped by $2,400, and the lease's next_due moved to 2026-10-02. The landlord was never contacted, which is correct.

Three agents were more than this needed. Between the Boss, Facilities and Accounting, the run called list_approvals 4 times, get_cash_position 3 times, get_shop_date 3 times and get_rent_due twice. That was 14 model requests and 37,061 tokens for one lookup and one queued payment. The answer was right. The coordination cost was out of proportion (question 3).

Ticket 103: Yale AI Club hoodies. Grade: partially successful, and the most realistic test of judgement.

The analysis was solid:

  • Inventory: found 8 navy size M hoodies against 20 requested, so 12 short and a $264 restock (12 × $22 unit cost). The apparel vendor was blocked by invoice 501.
  • Accounting: confirmed the $22 cost and $58 list price, and gave the boundary that discounts above 62.1% go below cost.
  • Boss: chose a sensible 10% rather than anything near that boundary: $52.20 each, $1,044 for 20, $30.20 margin per unit before other expenses. The Club gets meaningful value, and the shop keeps more than half of each sale as margin.

The first run (79129698ebb8) also queued approval #4, the $264 restock order, while #1 ($840), #2 ($8) and #3 ($2,400) were still pending. That made $3,512 of requests against $3,400 of cash. Accounting noticed and warned that pending approvals were $112 over. Each payment was affordable on its own; together they weren't. No agent could, or should, pick which one loses. I approved the invoice, the tee order and the rent, and rejected #4 because "only $152 would remain against $264", which is the same $112 gap. The first run also skipped Customer Service entirely, so the Club had no reply even though the Boss had decided on a discount.

The second run (78a13a51e4cf) is where the system showed it could adapt. It started from the new facts: $152 in checking, nothing pending, #4 rejected. Accounting concluded that the restock "cannot be queued or afforded now" and, importantly, didn't queue another one. The Boss kept the 10% offer and sent Customer Service to save draft 2. The draft offers the 8 available hoodies now and says plainly that the other 12 can't be confirmed or dated. That's honest, and it keeps the conversation going. But the Club asked for 20 and can currently get 8, and the ticket correctly stays in_progress in the database. The dashboard's RESOLVED stamp means "the crew's run finished". A completed agent run is not the same as a solved customer problem.

From a real-business angle: the 20-hoodie order would bring in $1,044, far more than the $264 restock. A real shop would ask the Club for a deposit to fund the restock. This model has no revenue (cash only goes out), and the agents correctly refused to count expected sales as money. So the right move inside these rules was the one they made: don't overcommit, offer what exists, and leave the funding decision to a human.

3. What would have been simpler with one agent

Ticket 102 is the clearest case. One agent with get_rent_due, get_cash_position, list_approvals and prepare_payment could do the whole job in about four tool calls:

  1. read the lease;
  2. read cash and pending commitments;
  3. confirm no duplicate is queued;
  4. queue the rent.
The multi-agent run took 14 model requests, 19 tool calls and 37,061 tokens over 56 seconds, mostly because every agent re-established context. Each one re-read the date, the cash and the queue, and every handoff re-sent the instructions and facts. Nothing in this ticket needed a second perspective. It needed one careful lookup and one queued payment.

Parts of 101 could merge too. Inventory and Accounting both called get_invoice_status(501), and the stock check and the overdue-invoice check are simple reads either agent could do. A single agent could have checked stock, found the blocked vendor, and queued the invoice payment in one pass, at well under the 71,047 tokens this run used. The Customer Service draft could also be written by that agent, though a dedicated writer with a narrower brief is a reasonable safety choice for customer-facing text.

Ticket 103 is where specialization paid off. The decision really had three sides:

  • Inventory knew what could physically be delivered: 8 now, 12 blocked.
  • Accounting knew what was affordable and profitable: the margins, the 62.1% cost boundary, and the $112 gap.
  • Customer Service turned the result into an honest offer.
The Boss's 10% decision was better for having those views side by side. I'd keep multiple agents where the expertise differs, and skip them where they only pass a message along. Even with one agent, I would not merge preparing and executing payments. The agent should still only queue. approve_payment should stay a human-only action, with its own balance and vendor checks.

4. Three new problems my current agents and tools could solve

  1. Another vendor bill blocking a customer order. Example: a customer orders a Crest Mug, and CC-MUG-CREST has 0 in stock.
    Sequence: Boss → Inventory (check_stock / check_bulk_order for the shortfall; list_vendors to match the mug to Elm City Gifts by specialty and see whether an open invoice blocks it) → Accounting (list_invoices, get_cash_position, list_approvals, then prepare_payment for the blocking invoice and prepare_purchase_order at the $3.50 unit cost) → Boss (update_ticket → waiting_approval) → Customer Service (save_customer_draft).
    Expected outcome: approvals queued for a human, delivery estimated with Elm City Gifts' 3-day lead time, and a draft with no firm date. That's the ticket 101 pattern with a different vendor.
  2. A new student organization's bulk request. Example: 30 Yale caps (CC-HAT-BLUE, size OS) with a discount.
    Sequence: Boss → Inventory (check_bulk_order: 40 on hand, so no restock) → Accounting (check_bulk_order with several discount_pct values; $6 cost and $22 list means a lot of room, with the cost boundary at about 72.7% off) → Boss decides → Customer Service drafts.
    Expected outcome: unlike ticket 103 this one can be fully met from stock, so it's a pure pricing decision with no cash outflow and no approvals. If the request were larger than stock, the ticket 103 logic (restock cost against cash) would take over.
  3. Next month's rent with other bills competing. Example: rent for lease 1 is next due 2026-10-02.
    Sequence: Boss → Facilities (get_rent_due(1): $2,400; days until due measured from desk.date_today) → Accounting (get_cash_position and list_approvals to compare with pending commitments; prepare_payment(rent) only if the balance covers it on its own).
    Expected outcome: with today's $152, prepare_payment would refuse ("cannot cover $2,400"). The agents would report the shortfall instead of queuing. That's useful: the tools prevent a negative balance, and the owner learns early that the shop can't make next month's rent without new money.

What I'm taking away

  • Actions, not summaries, are the unit of work. The failed 101 attempt "decided" to pay invoice 501 and changed nothing. I now judge a run by the rows it wrote (approvals, drafts, tickets), not by its final paragraph.
  • Payments that are each affordable can be unaffordable together. $840, $8, $2,400 and $264 each fit within $3,400. All four together came to $3,512. Checking each payment against the balance alone isn't enough. The queue as a whole matters.
  • Human approval was a real decision, not a formality. Choosing rent and the vendor invoice over the hoodie restock was a business priority call that no prompt should make silently. The code backed it up: it refused the purchase orders until invoice 501 was paid and would have refused anything that overdrew the account.
  • "Run finished" ≠ "customer served". Ticket 103's run finished with a stamp on the dashboard, while the database correctly keeps it in_progress, because 12 hoodies are still unfunded.
  • Specialize where expertise differs; don't delegate just to pass messages. 103 benefited from three viewpoints. 102 spent 37k tokens on a four-call job.
  • Good agent systems adapt to human decisions. After I rejected #4, the second 103 run read the new state, didn't re-queue the rejected order, and changed the offer to 8 hoodies now. That's the behaviour I want when the human and the agents disagree.
